Chapter One

The plot boundary is the financial foundation of every contract

Contract farming companies provide seeds and fertilizer to smallholder farmers on credit, based on the measured area of their land. If that measurement is wrong, the contract is wrong — and the consequences flow downstream to every seasonal decision.

An error of 0.1 hectares on a typical 1.5-hectare plot is a 7% discrepancy in contract value. Multiply that across thousands of farmers and 5–10 re-mapping visits per season, and small errors compound into serious money — for both sides.

The person doing the measuring maps 10–15 plots a day, walking each perimeter on foot — in direct sunlight at 35°C+, with sweaty or gloved hands, basic literacy, in Portuguese or regional languages. Completely offline: no internet, no basemap, no one to call.

Outcome first

Delivered the complete field-mapping experience — pre-survey readiness checks through boundary capture, GPS error recovery, obstacle annotation, supervisor review, and version overlay. Approved by the CTO for development. ~25 production-ready screens, design-system-compliant, with a verified PT-PT localization glossary.

Chapter Two

Five constraints that don't exist in normal design work

01No ground truthWithout satellite imagery, neither the system nor the technician can see the correct shape. GPS drifts 3–5m in the open and 8–15m under tree canopy. The app can only trust GPS points and the human walking on the land.
02The screen is the only mapPlots are irregular 8–20 vertex polygons with no visible markers. The technician can never see the shape they're drawing from above. The shape has to render progressively from nothing — with orientation cues — on a blank canvas.
03Sunlight, not showroom100% screen brightness. High contrast only. Minimum 48dp touch targets. Icon-first communication — because squinting at a label while walking in direct heat is a guaranteed interaction failure.
04Data can never be lostA battery death or app crash mid-walk must not cost a 30-minute perimeter walk. Autosave at every captured point isn't a nice-to-have — it's the contract.
05Behavior is a constraint tooReal incidents of technicians gaming previous tools — including one who walked the same plot nine times to register it for nine different farmers. The lesson became a design principle: if the honest path isn't the easiest path, technicians will find a workaround. Every guardrail exists because of a real story, not a persona.

Chapter Three

Pressure-testing assumptions when you can't reach the users

Technicians were unreachable in the field, so the process was built around weekly working sessions with the stakeholder — I brought assumptions and design ideas, and we stress-tested them together.

This is a single-stakeholder proxy, and it is the biggest methodological limitation of the work. What made it defensible was treating every stakeholder claim as a hypothesis to test — not a fact to build on. The table below is the paper trail. Some assumptions broke, some got sharpened, one got killed on the spot.

What I brought

What came out of it

Assumed technicians hold the tablet one-handed while walking

Broke. Any operation while walking is two-handed. Screen locked to portrait as a consequence.

Proposed a guided walk using visual + audio + haptic feedback

Validated and adopted — with one caveat: audio only where it doesn't drain battery, since field power is scarce.

Raised voice input as a way to cut typing burden

Ruled out together. Unreliable in these regions. Audio instructions are fine; voice input is not.

Explored sketch-first: farmer draws a rough shape to prime the walk

Killed. The client had run an experiment where office staff couldn't draw the shape of their own office after a year. Spatial memory doesn't work that way.

The favorite failure

Sketch-first was my strongest going-in hypothesis. It died in a single conversation — because we tested the assumption instead of the mockup. The discovery it left behind was more valuable than the idea itself: in this context, the walk is the interface. Everything else supports the walk.

Principle 01

The system suggests — it never asserts

The app has no ground truth. It flags anomalies and proposes actions — but the final call always belongs to the human standing on the land. A system that overrides the technician on data it can't fully trust will erode trust faster than bad GPS.

Principle 02

Reduce physical effort, relentlessly

Walking 10–15 perimeters a day is physically demanding. Shared boundary reuse, tap-to-mark on re-visits, targeted segment re-walks — physical effort is a UX metric here.

Chapter Four

Nine decisions, each solving a specific failure mode

Decision 01

Don't let a bad session start

A failed mapping session wastes 30 minutes of walking. Four gates must pass before the technician moves a step: GPS signal quality (HDOP threshold), battery level, farmer presence, and offline map availability. If a gate fails, the system blocks the start and says exactly what to fix.

Decision 02

Method selection — then the walk

With an offline map: choose Tap and Mark (faster) or Walk the Boundary (recommended). Without one, the walk is the only option.

Numbered markers (1, 2, 3…), not text labels — less to translate, less clutter while walking.

5-second GPS stabilization countdown on every capture.

Direction-change detection suggests marking a point; the technician confirms. (Suggest, never assert.)

Neighbor boundary reuse — never trace the same line twice.

Auto-save at every point — mid-walk battery death loses nothing.

Decision 03

When GPS lies — one action, not three

The hardest moment: GPS drifts 8–15m under mango trees and the boundary grows a bulge that doesn't exist. Early versions showed three recovery options — too many choices in 35°C heat. The final design detects the anomaly and nudges a single recommended action: re-walk to the last good point. Alternatives are tucked away. One decision. Clear. Fast.

Decision 04

Closing the loop — four methods, one favorite

Nobody walks back to the exact starting point. Four closure methods: straight-line, road-detection, shared-boundary, and manual. Shared-boundary close was the favorite — if a neighboring plot is already mapped, the system offers to reuse that edge. Physical effort reduced, data consistency improved.

Decision 05

Review and edit — with guardrails from real abuse cases

A preview mode lets technicians edit points — but the guardrails came from fraud stories, not UX heuristics.

Edits only while physically inside the plot — geofenced against adjustments from home or a truck.

Point movement capped at 5% — prevents subtle shape distortion that would pass a visual check.

Original shape always retained — one-tap reset at any point.

Decision 06

Obstacles — capture without interrupting the walk

Rocks, trees, ponds, and huts reduce cultivable area, but forcing full capture mid-walk breaks the primary task. The compromise: geotag a photo in one tap, annotate later. Large enclosed obstacles get their own perimeter walk using the same mechanics — rendered in teal so it never reads as a plot edge.

Decision 07

Supervisor review — not every rejection means re-walking

Supervisors can approve, reject with a typed reason (GPS drift, area mismatch, overlap, duplicate), or request a targeted re-walk of specific segments — not the whole perimeter. Rejections aren't uniform: some need data corrections, some escalation, some re-walking. The interface makes that distinction visible. Every destructive action gets a confirmation modal.

Decision 08

Versions and seasonal layers — see what changed, not just what is

A plot is mapped 5–10 times per season; every session is an immutable version. The review interface overlays current vs. prior boundaries with area deltas — comparing polygons visually is faster and more reliable than comparing hectares in a table when you're reviewing 40 submissions a day.

Decision 09

Language, localization, and design system — craft at the infrastructure level

A mistranslated button in the field has no support hotline behind it. I built an EN→PT-PT glossary verified for Mozambican field usage ("parcela" for plot, "limite" for boundary) and a canonical CTA vocabulary across 25+ screens. All screens migrated to a strict design system — no hardcoded colors, all components as library instances. A color-blind-safe variant replaced red/green status with a blue-primary scheme.

Chapter Five

Approved, shipped to development, and scoped for what comes next

Approved

Full experience approved for development

CTO and stakeholders signed off after the final demo. ~25 production-ready screens covering the happy path plus GPS drift, interruption, overlap, duplicate, and supervisor flows.

Design system

Compliant screen library

No hardcoded values, no freehand styles — all components as library instances. The product scales to new modules and markets without breaking visual consistency.

Localization

Icon-first UI + verified PT-PT glossary

Reduced translatable strings to a minimal, controlled vocabulary. Terms verified for Mozambican field usage — not just translated from English.

Roadmap

Full boundary walk → tap-to-mark on re-visits → AI-assisted boundary suggestions (future). The architecture for reducing technician effort season over season.

Chapter Six

What I learned from designing for an environment that fights back

01Design for the worst actor and the worst GPS — the honest user on a clear day gets a great experience for free. The 5% edit threshold, inside-boundary editing, and immutable version storage all came from real fraud and error stories, not theoretical abuse cases. Designing to the edge case made the whole system more trustworthy.
02Killing your own idea early is cheap. Sketch-first died in one conversation — because the stakeholder had already run the experiment. Testing the assumption instead of the mockup saved weeks of design work on something that would never have landed in the field.
03In low-trust sensing environments, humility is a UI pattern. "The system suggests, the human decides" is the only epistemically defensible stance for an app with no satellite imagery, drifting GPS, and a technician who is the only one who can see the actual ground.
04Get to the field earlier. Every stakeholder working session was valuable — but it was proxy. Two days shadowing a technician would have replaced weeks of assumption-testing. The next version of this project starts in the field.

The principle this work left me with

Physical effort is a UX metric. When the interface is a 10" tablet held in both hands, in the sun, while walking uneven farmland — every tap saved is a real reduction in cognitive and physical load. These aren't convenience features. They're the difference between a tool technicians adopt and one they work around.
