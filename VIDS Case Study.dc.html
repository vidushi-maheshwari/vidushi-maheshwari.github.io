Real-time incident detection for a 200 km highway corridor

Chapter One

AI cameras watching 200 km of highway. No structured system to act on what they were seeing.

The same ATMS platform that digitised the Emergency Control Box workflow also covered video incident detection. Each VIDS device is a cluster mounted on a single pole: three cameras covering three directions, one radar, and one LPU (Local Processing Unit) handling edge-level detection before sending data to the control room. The cameras were doing the detection. What was missing was a control room interface capable of making sense of what they were seeing.

Control room operators — working 24/7 shifts — had no structured view of device health, incident volume per location, or live alert status across the corridor. Their job: monitor the dashboard, track incident counts per device and package, keep a live check on ongoing tickets and critical alerts, and compile reports for higher officials. Without a coherent interface, that job came down to memory and manual checking.

For the first time, operators needed to —

01See which VIDS devices were operational at the start of every shift — before trusting any of their detection outputs
02Drill into any device and see its three camera feeds, radar, and LPU status together — not as separate systems
03Track incident counts from specific devices within a package to identify where problems were clustering
04Keep a live view of current incidents, ongoing tickets, and critical alerts without switching screens
05Have enough structured data to compile shift reports for higher officials

The goal

Design the VIDS module for NHAI's ATMS platform — a single interface to monitor device health, track incident counts by device and package, stay across open tickets, and generate the data operators needed to report upward. The whole system had to be readable at a shift glance, not investigated piece by piece.

Chapter Two

Designing for operators who think in km markers — not camera IDs

01

PRD deep-dive

6 incident detection types, device architecture per pole (3 cameras + radar + LPU), package groupings, alert severity levels, and the control room's monitoring + reporting scope

02

Competitive benchmarking

Logipix HIGS for spatial orientation via reference-point maps; Vehant VIDS/VSDS for co-presenting multi-sensor data in a single incident view

03

Heuristic evaluation

Adapted for safety-critical control rooms — visual salience, spatial mental model alignment, and cognitive load reduction couldn't be traded off against each other

04

Operator context mapping

Who was in the control room, what their shift looked like, and what "done" meant — monitoring and reporting upward, not incident dispatch

The most important research finding wasn't about features — it was about mental models. Operators don't think in camera IDs or VIDS numbers. They think in km markers, directions, and package locations — the same spatial logic they use when coordinating with patrol on the ground. A system that displayed incidents as "VIDS783 — Camera 2" was structurally wrong for the people using it, regardless of how well the detection worked.

That finding shaped three decisions: alert cards use plain-language package locations, the device detail panel names feeds by direction (East / South / West) — not camera number, and the map renders the corridor as a road layout anchored to package locations, so operators orient spatially the same way they'd talk about the highway in person.

Control room journey — a shift in the life of a VIDS operator

01

Checks VIDS device health across the corridor

02

Drills into device detail — camera feeds, radar, LPU status

03

Tracks incident counts per device per package · monitors live alerts, tickets, critical events

The output of this shift: a structured report for higher officials — incident volumes by location, device health status, alert history. The dashboard had to make that data readable without manual aggregation.

A thing I got wrong early

My initial framing treated the alert queue as the core of the operator's job — as if the primary task was responding to individual incidents in real time. It isn't. The primary task is monitoring across the shift and building a picture accurate enough to report upward. That changes what the dashboard prioritises: not just the loudest alert, but a clear read on incident counts, ticket status, and whether the devices generating those counts are actually healthy. I reframed the whole information hierarchy around that job.

Device Details view — VIDS number, location, individual device health status per camera and LPU

Chapter Three

Deciding what the dashboard shows — and in what order

The PRD specified the data the system could expose. The design question was which of that data earned permanent, always-visible real estate — and which belonged one level deeper. The answer came from a single question for each data type: does an operator need this before they can act, or after they've already decided to investigate?

Anything that answered "before" went on the dashboard. Anything "after" went inside the device detail panel. That filter produced four dashboard-level data types with a specific vertical order — plus one platform-provided surface held in the persistent right panel.

01

First — System health

Can I trust what this dashboard is showing me?

If VIDS systems are offline, every gap in incident detection from those zones is a false negative — not a quiet highway. Health had to be the first thing visible on every session, without a click.

Rendered as: operational/total donut + device list with online/offline status per VIDS ID

02

Second — Live vehicle volume

What's the baseline load on the highway right now?

Volume by vehicle type sets the interpretive context for incident rates. 20,000 vehicles with a high truck share is a different risk exposure than 20,000 mostly cars. Operators needed this before looking at incident counts.

Rendered as: vehicle trend donut — All / Car / Bus / Truck · total count at centre

03

Third — Traffic trends by location

Where on the highway is volume anomalous?

A spike that happens at the same location every day at the same hour is a road design issue. A spike that's never appeared before is an active incident. That distinction changes the response — the 12-hour line chart makes it visible.

Rendered as: multi-line chart — X-axis: VIDS location · Y-axis: traffic count · last 12 hours

04

Fourth — Incident trends by type

What kind of incidents are clustering, and where?

Incident count by type distinguishes operational load from safety risk. 150 congestion incidents and 0 wrong-direction incidents is a different operational picture than one generating both — visible at dashboard level, no drill-down required.

Rendered as: multi-line chart — 6 incident types · X-axis: VIDS location · last 12 hours

05

Persistent right panel — Platform alert queue

Where the platform's alert queue sits in the VIDS view

The alert queue is a platform-wide surface, not something built for VIDS. What was in scope was where it sat: the persistent right panel keeps it visible everywhere without competing with the health, incident, and spatial context doing the interpretive work.

Rendered as: persistent right-side panel — platform-provided widget, positioned within the VIDS layout

The decision that wasn't obvious

The first version put the alert queue at the top — it's what operators act on most urgently. But an operator can't correctly interpret a quiet alert queue if they don't know whether the systems generating the incidents behind it are running. A silent alert queue on a system that's 30% offline is not good news — it's a blind spot. Health had to come first.

Chapter Four

Four features, each mapped to a gap in the operator workflow

Every decision was traceable to a specific failure mode in the existing workflow. The target user set the constraints: operators working long shifts, under stress, with variable network reliability and limited training time.

Feature 01 · VIDS Health Dashboard

Know what's working before you trust what it's reporting

The dashboard opens on a system health view: a donut indicator shows how many VIDS systems are operational alongside a device list grouped by VIDS ID. Each device's online/offline status is visible at a glance, alongside vehicle trend data across all active systems.

Donut indicator shows operational vs. total VIDS count — a single number that tells operators what to trust before the shift starts.

Device list groups each VIDS ID with its package location and live status — no manual health check required.

Vehicle trend donut (All / Car / Bus / Truck) gives a real-time volume snapshot across the full corridor.

VIDS Systems Health — operational count donut · Vehicle trends last 12 hours

Feature 02 · Traffic & Incident Trend Charts

See the pattern before it becomes a crisis

Two real-time line charts — traffic volume by vehicle type and incident counts by type — track data across all VIDS locations over the last 12 hours. Operators can identify which package is generating disproportionate incidents and contextualise a single alert within the broader trend without leaving the dashboard — exactly the kind of pattern that needs to land in the shift report.

Traffic trends: All / Car / Bus / Truck across VIDS locations — volume distribution and anomalies against baseline.

Incidents recorded: Wrong Direction / Traffic / Slow Moving / Fallen Object / Congestion / Stopped Vehicle — where incident types are clustering.

Time window dropdown (Last 12 hours) shifts the view without navigating away from the overview.

Traffic trends and incidents recorded — by VIDS location, last 12 hours

Feature 03 · Device Detail Panel

All five components of a VIDS pole — visible together in one panel

Clicking a device opens a detail panel showing the full pole cluster together — online status, plain-language location, camera thumbnails per direction, LPU health, and a live table of passing vehicles with type and timestamp. Operators assess the full health of a device and the activity it's seeing without opening a separate screen for each component.

Three directional camera feeds (East / South / West) in one panel — full spatial coverage of the pole, no view switching.

LPU health (Working fine / Unhealthy) at device level — if the LPU is unhealthy, its detections can't be trusted; operators know before including that device's data in a report.

Live passing vehicle log (vehicle type + timestamp, LIVE indicator) — the data source operators use when tracking counts for shift reports.

VIDS 34 detail — 3-direction cameras · radar · LPU status · live passing vehicle log

Feature 04 · Highway Map View

Spatial orientation — where on the highway, not just which camera

A toggle switches the VIDS Devices section from list view to a highway map: the corridor rendered as a road layout — LHS and RHS lanes — with VIDS positions plotted at their actual package locations. Green and red dot indicators show device health at a glance, so operators can see which package is affected and whether the surrounding devices are operational.

Highway layout (LHS / RHS) mirrors the spatial logic operators already use — km-based, directional, anchored to package references, not abstract camera IDs.

Package groupings (Package 44 Tonk, Package 45 Brijlaalpura) give incidents geographic context.

List / Map toggle shifts between data detail and spatial overview without a separate navigation path.

Map view — VIDS positions along LHS/RHS corridor · Package 44 & 45 · health indicators

Note on identifiers: package numbers and location labels shown throughout this study are illustrative placeholders, not the actual package numbers marked on the highway. The design pattern is real; the identifiers are anonymised.

Chapter Five

What the dashboard hierarchy made possible — and what's still open

Shipped

VIDS module delivered end-to-end as part of the NHAI ATMS platform — health-first hierarchy, incident and traffic trend charts, unified device detail panel, and highway map view all in production scope.

Still open

Operator-side validation. Internal walkthrough only — no real operator sessions. The hierarchy is defensible on reasoning; the ergonomic proof is the next chapter, and it's what I'd fix first.

Before the VIDS module, device health was checked separately, incident counts required manual aggregation across logs, and there was no way to see the full corridor spatially. Shift reports were built from memory and manual cross-referencing. This interface replaced that process.

01

Device health readable at shift start

Operators could see which devices were operational before relying on their outputs — gaps in incident data could be explained, not misread as a quiet corridor.

02

Incident counts per device, per package

The dashboard surfaced how many incidents each device within a package had recorded — the structured data operators needed to track patterns and compile reports.

03

Full pole detail in one panel

All five components — three directional cameras, radar, and LPU — together. Operators assessed the whole pole without switching between views.

04

Live vehicle data for real-time tracking

The live passing vehicle log gave a timestamped record of activity per device — the ground-level data that fed into shift reports for higher officials.

05

Spatial view of the corridor

The map view placed devices in their actual highway positions by package — operators could see where incident clusters were forming, not just which device ID was flagging.

Internal walkthrough — what it revealed

During the walkthrough, the PM clicked through the East, South, and West camera feeds. A senior stakeholder immediately asked: "what happens when LPU is unhealthy?" Asked unprompted in the first five minutes, that meant the primary flow had landed clearly enough that stakeholders had moved on to edge cases. It also surfaced a gap: unhealthy-state UI for individual pole components needed a more explicit pass — something a real operator session would have caught. The absence of one is the thing I'd change first.

Chapter Six

What I'd approach differently

01Design the output before designing the interface. The operator's real job was producing shift reports — but the report format was never defined in the brief. If the report template had been defined first, the dashboard hierarchy would have been easier to justify: every data type that needed to land in the report earned real estate; everything else went in the drill-down.
02Design the false positive feedback loop on day one. Rural highways generate high false positive rates — animals, tractors, dust, shadows. "Mark as false alert" with structured reason codes was in scope, but the feedback mechanism that would tune the AI detection over time was deferred. It should have been core to the initial design.
03Push harder for Hindi labels in the operator interface. The PRD was in English; the interface was designed in English. But the operators using it every day were more comfortable in Hindi — particularly under stress. Language was treated as a localisation concern rather than a core usability one. I'd challenge that earlier and more forcefully.
04Map view mental model needed local landmarks, not package IDs. Operators on the ground navigate by km markers, village names, and landmarks. Package IDs were an internal taxonomy — not the one operators would use to communicate incident locations to patrol. That gap should have been caught in research.

Chapter Seven

One module in a 10+ module platform — its device patterns influenced every module that followed

VIDS sat inside the same ATMS platform as the ECB emergency call system — Command Center 4, covering the 200 km Gurgaon–Dausa stretch. Operators moved between modules in a single session: ECB for emergency calls, VIDS for incident detection, VSDS for speed violations, TMCS for traffic flow, Ticket Management for response tracking. The device detail panel pattern established here became a reusable structure across the platform — the same information-first, health-visible-first hierarchy carried into other module surfaces.

A note on the ECB case study: ECB is the sibling module from this same engagement, written up separately because the design problem is genuinely different — agent workflow for one-to-one emergency calls, not operator dashboards for continuous multi-stream monitoring. Treat them as one engagement with two modules. ECB set the platform's design vocabulary; VIDS extended it into the monitoring surface.

What this phase taught me

Designing for safety-critical operational environments is fundamentally different from designing for task completion. The question isn't "can the user find what they need?" — it's "will the user see what matters most when they're under the most pressure?" Health-first hierarchy, spatial mental model alignment, and one-screen incident resolution weren't nice-to-haves. They were the only choices that made sense for operators working 12-hour shifts watching a highway that doesn't pause.
