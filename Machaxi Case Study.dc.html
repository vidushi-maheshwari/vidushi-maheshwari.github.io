Machaxi Learn

Reshaping a coaching product from plan management into a full learning lifecycle

Machaxi Learn had solid plan-management utilities — curriculum tracking, compensation booking, membership pausing. What it didn't have was a front door: a way to help new users understand the offering, choose with confidence, and build a reason to come back. I redesigned it end to end.

Chapter One

A product optimised for people who had already decided

Machaxi Learn is a structured coaching product — badminton, swimming, and other sports delivered through curriculum-based plans at partner facilities. When I joined the redesign, it was functional but inward-facing. If you were already enrolled, it handled your needs well: curriculum, schedule, membership pausing, leave compensation.

If you weren't enrolled yet — or were considering switching plans — the product offered almost nothing. No clear entry point explaining the coaching model. No decision support for choosing the right sport, level, or plan. No framing that made Learn feel like a structured improvement program rather than a scheduling app. The gap wasn't in the features. It was in the product's framing of itself.

The structural problem

Learn was evolving in parallel toward an AI-supported coaching model — performance tracking, technique analysis, and feedback loops becoming core parts of the product. The existing interface couldn't support that ambition. There was no experience layer designed to make those AI outputs feel credible, actionable, or tied to the user's personal progress. The coaching data existed; the product didn't translate it into anything meaningful.

Chapter Two

Five gaps in the legacy experience

This wasn't a failing product — the existing utilities were real and used. But the structure of the experience had created five compounding gaps.

01

No entry point for new users

The product assumed familiarity. There was no page, flow, or framing that introduced Learn as a structured coaching product — what it was, how it worked, or why it was worth the commitment.

02

Zero decision support at the top of the funnel

Users could manage existing plans but received no guidance when choosing a sport, proficiency level, schedule type, or plan tier. The purchase entry was effectively a form, not a flow.

03

Generic plan logic across different sports

A badminton plan and a swimming plan don't work the same way — in structure, in pricing, in scheduling. The product treated them identically, creating confusion at exactly the moment users needed clarity.

04

Utility-first framing post-enrollment

Once enrolled, the product felt like an admin dashboard. Attendance, schedule, compensation — all useful. But nothing surfaced progress, improvement, or a reason to care about the next session beyond keeping track of the current one.

05

AI outputs had nowhere to land

Performance data, technique scores, error detection — being generated but with no designed home in the interface. It surfaced as data, not insight. Users couldn't tell what it meant, what to do about it, or whether it should change how they trained.

These weren't independent problems. They were symptoms of a product designed around enrolled users that hadn't yet built an experience for anyone becoming one.

Multi-sport complexity

Each sport had distinct plan structures, scheduling logic, and expectations. The design had to accommodate this without creating five separate products.

Operational PRD depth

Player selection, skill level, time slots, center, sessions per week, fixed vs. custom schedules, discounts, validity — all needed surface area without collapsing into a form.

Mixed user states

First-time users evaluating the product and existing members managing plans had to share one interface without either experience being compromised.

AI comprehension gap

Insights couldn't feel abstract or technical. The product had to translate data into legible, credible, actionable takeaways for everyday learners — not analysts.

Chapter Three

Three product shifts that defined the redesign

The redesign wasn't a screen refresh. The goal was to reorganise the experience around the user's critical moments — understanding the product, choosing a path with confidence, purchasing without friction, and returning often enough to build a habit. That produced three core shifts.

Before

Helps users manage a plan they're already on

After

Guides users from discovery through enrollment and into an ongoing coaching loop

Before

Generic membership logic across all sports

After

Sport-specific purchase journeys that match how each sport is actually bought and used

Before

Passive servicing layer post-enrollment

After

Active coaching experience with visible progress, feedback, and AI-supported improvement signals

These shifts guided every major design decision — what got priority on screen, what got staged behind a drill-down, and how the product communicated its own value to someone who hadn't paid yet.

Directions I considered and moved off

Before locking these three shifts, three alternative directions got serious consideration and were dropped. The shifts only hold up because these were rejected.

01
One universal purchase flow across all sports
Faster to build and consistent with how most B2C plan products work. Dropped once the sport-specific mental models became clear: badminton is a recurring weekly plan; swimming is sessions with a validity window. A single flow would have forced either sport into a shape it doesn't fit — and the AI layer needs sport-specific grounding to feel credible.
02
Surface AI outputs as raw performance data
The temptation with new AI features is to show off — confidence scores, breakdowns, technical categories. Dropped because raw data reads as noise to a casual learner and creates the opposite of trust. The AI generates the insight; the design translates it into a coaching signal with a next step. Chose editorial layer over raw model output.
03
Keep the existing home screen as a management dashboard
The utilities were real and working. The safe move was to leave them front-and-centre and add a new-user landing page on top. Dropped because it would have preserved the "admin console" feel that was the underlying problem. The utilities stayed in the product; they just stopped being the first thing anyone sees.

Chapter Four

Six decisions that changed how the product works

Each decision was grounded in one of the three product shifts and targeted a specific failure point in the old experience.

Decision 01

Start with the learner, not the membership

The old experience assumed too much familiarity at the start. The redesigned flow begins with context: login, player creation, sport selection, goals, location, and proficiency. This reframe made the product feel personal from the first screen, and gave the system everything it needed to make downstream coaching recommendations feel grounded rather than generic. Because Learn often supports multiple players and family accounts, starting with the player also simplified navigation for returning users managing more than one person.

Onboarding — starts with the learner, not the membership

Decision 02

Adapt the purchase journey to sport-specific mental models

Badminton and swimming are bought differently. Swimming maps naturally to sessions and validity windows; badminton works more like a recurring weekly plan. Instead of one purchase model across all sports, the architecture was designed to match how each sport is actually evaluated, scheduled, and committed to. This wasn't a UX nicety — it was necessary for the AI coaching layer to feel credible. Generic coaching signals to someone buying a sport-specific plan read as noise. The sport-specific purchase structure grounded everything that followed.

Sport landing page — coaching benefits, testimonials, plan tiers, trial entry

Decision 03

Build conviction before asking for commitment

Users weren't just buying access — they were buying into a routine, a time commitment, and a belief that the product would actually improve their game. The redesigned sport landing pages surfaced coaching benefits, expected outcomes, workshop previews, testimonials, plan structures, and trial entry points before any purchase CTA. This shifted the product from utility-led to value-led: the page had to earn the click, not just receive it. Whether it moves conversion is a post-launch question the redesign was built to answer, not one I can claim it did.

Building conviction — benefits, testimonials, AI analytics preview, community, and trial entry

Decision 04

Make pricing and plan details explicitly clear before payment

In structured programs, ambiguity at checkout is fatal to conversion. Start dates, schedule summaries, applied discounts, and final costs all need to be visibly confirmed before the payment action — not discoverable only after clicking through. The redesigned checkout summarized the chosen player, sport, level, center, schedule, any discounts, and the exact start date on a single review screen. That transparency was also necessary foundation for the AI coaching features: users can only trust AI feedback if they already trust the product that delivers it.

Checkout review — player, sport, center, schedule, discount, start date, total

Decision 05

Shift post-purchase from servicing to engagement

The existing operational features — compensation booking, leave management, curriculum, rewards — were kept and needed to be. But they were repositioned from the product's front face to one layer of a broader post-enrollment experience. The new home state shows upcoming sessions, attendance streaks, active curriculum milestones, and coach feedback — the things that make a user feel progress, not just feel administered. The utilities remained; their hierarchy changed.

Post-enrollment home — sessions, attendance, curriculum progress, coach feedback

Decision 06

Make progress visible — and make AI the means, not the point

The most significant addition to the post-enrollment experience was the analytics and feedback layer: overall performance scores, technique breakdowns by category, detected errors, video-linked analysis clips, and specific improvement drills. The design challenge wasn't surfacing the data — it was translating it into something a casual badminton learner could act on without a coaching background. The principle: AI generates the insight, the design makes it legible. A wrong-direction alert with a drill recommendation reads as coaching. A raw confidence score does not.

AI insight layer — performance score, technique breakdown, error detection, improvement drills

Chapter Six

What I'd do differently — and where I'd push further

01Push for sport-specific user research earlier, not just PRD reading. The sport-specific purchase logic was one of the strongest decisions — but it came from reasoning through the PRD and sport mechanics, not from observing how real swimmers versus badminton players think about buying a plan. A two-session research sprint with buyers across two sports would have surfaced mental-model differences I had to infer, and likely revealed edge cases in the plan logic before they were built.
02Design the AI output layer before designing the screens it lives in. The analytics and feedback features were the strongest differentiator — and the hardest to get right, because "translate AI output into coaching insight" is an editorial problem as much as a UX problem. Knowing earlier what the system could actually say, and to what confidence level, would have changed how I scoped the feedback cards. I designed the container before fully understanding the content.
03Scope the in-between-sessions touchpoints as core, not future-phase. Retention for a coaching product is largely determined outside the facility — what happens between sessions. Post-session nudges, improvement reminders, and lightweight check-ins were treated as a next phase. For a product where long-term commitment is the whole value proposition, those moments should have been designed alongside the core flows, not deferred.
04Validate the pre-purchase conviction layer with actual non-users. The sport landing pages were designed to shift the product from utility-led to value-led — but every review happened with stakeholders who already understood the product. A five-person unmoderated test with people who'd never heard of Machaxi Learn would have told us whether the conviction-building content was working for the audience it needed to work on.

The core principle this work reinforced

Product maturity isn't always about adding functionality — it's often about reframing what you're asking the user to do. Learn already had the features. What it needed was a coherent story about what those features added up to, told in the right order, to the right people, at the right moment in their journey. That's a product problem, not a UI problem. The UX work was the mechanism; the real decision was about what Learn was going to claim about itself.

Platform Context

Learn had to work as a system, not a single screen — across sports, facilities, and user roles

Learn wasn't one product with one flow. It had to hold multiple sports (badminton, swimming, and others) with different plan structures and mental models, multiple partner facilities each with their own scheduling constraints, and multiple user roles on the same account — the learner, the parent booking on their behalf, and the coach delivering the sessions. The redesign had to work at all three levels simultaneously.

The decisions that made this possible were structural, not surface-level: the player identity system that let a single account manage multiple learners without the app collapsing into confusion; the sport-specific purchase logic that treated each sport as its own journey; and the AI insight layer designed to translate raw performance data into coaching signal regardless of which sport the data came from. Designing Learn as a system — not just a set of screens — is what made cross-sport, cross-facility consistency possible.
